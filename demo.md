# Warden — the demo, end to end

**The single working doc for the video.** Replaces `demo-brief.md` and `demo-video.md`.

`prd.md` is the business argument. `trd.md` is the build spec. This is what we're filming and how.

> **Deliverable: a recorded video we submit. Not a live stage demo.**
> This is the most important line in the document, and it changes everything downstream — see §7. Where `trd.md` assumes a live performance with a 9:30 PM backup recording, **this file wins.**

---

## 1. The idea in one sentence

A building owner pays a guard company about $200,000 a year to watch one property. Warden does that job with AI cameras instead — it watches, it talks to intruders, it calls the manager, and it writes the legal paperwork afterwards.

## 2. The problem, plainly

When a rental unit sits empty between tenants, that's when it gets broken into. Squatters move in, copper pipes get stolen. Nobody's watching, because you don't pay a guard to sit outside an empty apartment.

Two things make it worse:

1. **Alarms are noise.** A motion alarm says "something moved." It can't tell a raccoon from a delivery driver from someone prying a door. So people ignore them.
2. **Getting a squatter out takes months.** Courts need proof. Insurance needs proof. Most systems hand you a grainy clip and nothing else.

Warden fixes both: it says *what is actually happening*, then produces a document a court or an adjuster would accept.

---

## 3. What we're building — five pieces

### 3.1 The eye — the camera loop

A webcam watches a "property." Software compares each frame to the one before. When enough pixels change, something moved — and only then do we spend money on AI.

At that moment it grabs **three photos about half a second apart** and sends them to Claude. Three instead of one, because one photo can only say "a person is there." Three can say "that person is *trying the door handle*." **That difference is the whole product.**

Claude returns structured data, not prose: is there a person, how many, what are they doing, and a **threat level 1–5**.

| Level | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 1 | Nobody, or an obviously authorized worker in uniform |
| 2 | Someone walking past, touching nothing |
| 3 | Someone touching the building, or lingering with no reason |
| 4 | Someone actively trying to get in |
| 5 | They're inside, or someone is in danger |

### 3.2 The brain — knowing which properties matter tonight

Every property management system already knows which units are empty, so nobody "arms" anything. The setting follows the lease:

- Someone lives there → relaxed, alert at threat 4+
- Notice given, unit empty, or construction site → on guard, alert at threat 3+

The dashboard shows six properties. The star is **Maple Grove, Unit 4B** — empty 12 days between tenants. Empty means on guard, so **threat 3 is enough to trigger everything.**

### 3.3 The voice — talking the intruder out

The moment threat clears the bar, a speaker at the property says:

> "This property is monitored. Security has been notified. Leave the premises now."

Most people leave. That's the cheapest possible outcome and the whole reason this beats a camera that only records.

Voice generated by **Miso One** (sponsor). **Lines are rendered ahead of time and played as audio files** — nothing to go wrong while filming.

### 3.4 The phone call — a human makes the decision

If the intruder doesn't leave, the on-call manager's actual phone rings. A real call through **Phonely** (sponsor). The AI describes the scene and offers three choices:

> "Press one to send a guard. Press two to notify police. Press three to dismiss."

The manager picks one. That choice appears on the dashboard instantly.

This is what makes Warden a *service* and not software. We don't send an alert and hope. We call a human and get a decision.

If they pick "send a guard," we check through **VOYGR** (sponsor) that the security company is a real, currently-operating business — dispatching to a vendor that closed last month is a genuine failure mode.

### 3.5 The paperwork — the actual moat

One click produces a printable report that looks nothing like the dashboard: black on white, serif, deliberately boring, like something filed with a court.

- Which property and unit, who owns it, who manages it
- **Lease status, prominently:** "vacant, turnover day 12 of 21" — the line that matters in an eviction
- The photos, each stamped with exact time *and timezone*, plus a hash proving the image wasn't edited
- What the AI said, in its own raw words, not our summary
- Every action and who took it — the system, or the named manager logged in
- How it ended, and a signed statement that the record was created automatically at the time of the event

> *"This document is how a squatter gets evicted and how an insurance claim gets paid."*

Everyone else sells cameras. We hand you the evidence.

## 4. Why this wins as a business — 30 seconds

Every incident ends with a label: false alarm, left after the warning, manager dismissed, guard sent, police called, eviction filed, claim paid.

Nobody else has those labels. Camera companies see pixels and never learn what happened next. We see the ending, because we handle it.

Those labels are worth three things: they teach the system when to bother a human (literally our margin), a competitor can't copy them, and eventually they're the dataset that lets insurers price this risk.

> *"Competitors see pixels. Because we deliver the service, we see outcomes — and outcomes are what train the model, set the margin, and price the insurance."*

| 4 | The detection | Screen | 0:15 | Red sweep, then the threat card: `[FILL: level]` — `[FILL: behavior string]` |

| 7 | The report | Screen | 0:20 | Print preview; slow scroll through photos, timestamps, raw AI output, signature block |
| 8 | Close, presenter to camera | Camera | 0:20 | — |

**Narration:**

- **1** → *"A 24/7 security guard post costs about two hundred thousand dollars a year. Here's what replaces it."*
- **2** → *"Right now we're watching forty thousand doors. This one — Maple Grove, Unit 4B — has been empty for twelve days."*
- **4** → **Say nothing for one full second and let the card land.** Then: *"That's not a scripted alert. That's Claude looking at the actual frames and describing what it sees."*
- **5** → *(silence — let the deterrent play)*
- **6** → *"We don't hand you an alert. We make the decision."*
- **7** → *"Timestamped frames. Every action logged. This document is how a squatter gets evicted and how an insurance claim gets paid."*
- **8** → The moat line from §4, then: *"That was a two-hundred-thousand-dollar-a-year guard post, replaced. Now imagine forty thousand doors."*

**Two shots people rush.** Shot 5 — rehearse the freeze-and-back-away; it's the most satisfying beat in the video. Shot 7 — the PDF is the moat made visible, so scroll slowly enough to read section headers and hold three seconds on the attestation block.

**Film the intruder ourselves.** No footage from the internet: it's someone else's copyright, someone else's real crime, and a judge who recognizes the clip stops believing everything else.

## 6. Production notes

**Screen capture:** QuickTime → New Screen Recording, or `Cmd+Shift+5`.

> **Gotcha:** macOS screen recording does **not** capture system audio without a virtual audio device. Don't install one tonight. **Select the built-in microphone** and let it pick up the voice-down through the speakers. Slightly roomy, entirely adequate, zero setup risk. For shots where audio matters (5 and 6), film with a second phone instead.

**Second phone for anything physical.** Shots 3, 5, 6, and 8. It's the only source that captures the phone actually ringing and being answered — the moment a judge remembers. Prop it at laptop height, landscape, lock exposure and focus before rolling.

**Start a screen recorder early and leave it running.** We'll accidentally capture our best take of the pipeline working while testing it.

**Before filming:**

- [ ] Camera loop produces real threat cards from our own staged footage
- [ ] Phone call connects and the answer reaches the dashboard
- [ ] Report renders from a real incident, **no "localhost" in the page margins** (uncheck Headers and footers in the Chrome print dialog — it persists)
- [ ] Audio audible in a test recording, played back with sound
- [ ] Every on-screen timestamp shows its timezone
- [ ] No emoji anywhere in the interface
- [ ] Dashboard reset to a clean state, event stream empty

**After filming:** play the cut back start to finish with sound before calling it done. A recording nobody watched is not a deliverable. Save locally, not cloud-only.

## 7. How the recorded format changes things — all in our favor

1. **Record each piece the moment it works.** Nothing has to succeed all at once in one nerve-wracking take.
2. **Unlimited takes** of the flaky parts — the intruder walking in, the phone ringing.
3. **Narration recorded separately** in a quiet spot beats talking over a noisy room.
4. **The one thing we can't fake is the AI output.** Real JSON arriving from Claude on screen is more convincing than any mockup — and it's not extra work, it's recording what we're already testing.

**What this retires from `trd.md`:** the 9:30 PM freeze-and-record-a-backup ritual, the `T`/`D` on-stage determinism keys as *performance* insurance, and the Callwright go/no-go gate in §5.1 (Phonely now carries the call; VOYGR does vendor validation). The keyboard fallbacks are still worth having as *filming* conveniences — they just aren't load-bearing anymore.

## 8. What's real and what's fake — we say this out loud

Being straight about this is not a weakness. A judge who catches an unlabeled fake stops trusting the whole demo. A judge who watches us label it ourselves reads it as confidence.

**Real:** the camera loop and AI classification (genuinely live, on our own footage) · the phone call, to an actual phone · the vendor check through VOYGR · login and operator identity through Hexclave · the voice from Miso One · the report, generated from real incident data.

**Simulated, and labeled on screen:** the property-management feed — the logic turning lease status into alert sensitivity is real code, the connection to Yardi is not · actually dispatching a guard — we validate the vendor is real, then stop.

**Not claimed at all:** we have not trained a model on outcome labels. We built the system that *captures* them. If asked — *"Tonight we built the thing that produces the training data. The training set is what month one of the service produces."*

Any statistic marked `[verify]` in `prd.md` does not get spoken in the video.
