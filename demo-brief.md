# Warden — what we're building, in plain words

This is the simple version. `prd.md` is the business argument, `trd.md` is the build spec. Read this one first to understand the shape of the thing.

**Deliverable: a recorded video demo that we submit.** Not a live stage demo. That matters, and it's mostly good news — see "How the video changes things" at the bottom.

---

## The idea in one sentence

A building owner pays a security guard company about $200,000 a year to watch one property. Warden does that job with AI cameras instead — it watches, it talks to intruders, it calls the manager, and it writes the legal paperwork afterwards.

## The problem, plainly

When a rental unit is empty between tenants, that's when it gets broken into. Squatters move in, copper pipes get stolen. Nobody is watching, because you don't pay a guard to sit outside an empty apartment.

Two things make that worse:

1. **Alarms are noise.** A motion alarm tells you "something moved." It doesn't tell you if it's a raccoon, a delivery driver, or someone prying a door open. So people ignore them.
2. **Getting a squatter out takes months.** Courts need proof. Insurance needs proof. Most systems give you a grainy clip and nothing else.

Warden fixes both. It looks at the footage and says *what is actually happening*. Then it produces a document that a court or an insurance adjuster would accept.

## What we're actually building — five pieces

### 1. The eye (the camera loop)

A webcam watches a "property." Software compares each frame to the one before it. When enough pixels change, something moved — and only then do we spend money on AI.

At that moment it grabs three photos, about half a second apart, and sends them to Claude. Three photos instead of one, because one photo can only tell you "a person is there." Three photos can tell you "that person is *trying the door handle*." That difference is the whole product.

Claude sends back structured data, not a paragraph: is there a person, how many, what are they doing, and a **threat level from 1 to 5**.

- 1 = nobody, or an obviously authorized worker in a uniform
- 2 = someone walking past, not touching anything
- 3 = someone touching the building, or hanging around with no reason
- 4 = someone actively trying to get in
- 5 = they're inside, or someone is in danger

### 2. The brain (knowing which properties matter tonight)

Every property management system already knows which units are empty. So we don't ask anyone to "arm" anything — the setting follows the lease automatically:

- Someone lives there → we're relaxed, only alert at threat 4+
- Tenant gave notice, or the unit is empty, or it's a construction site → we're on guard, alert at threat 3+

Our dashboard shows six properties. The star of the demo is **Maple Grove, Unit 4B** — empty for 12 days between tenants. Empty means on guard, so threat 3 is enough to trigger everything.

### 3. The voice (talking the intruder out)

The moment the threat is high enough, a speaker at the property speaks:

> "This property is monitored. Security has been notified. Leave the premises now."

Most people leave at this point. That's the cheapest possible outcome, and it's the whole reason this beats a camera that just records.

The voice is generated by **Miso One** — a sponsor's AI voice model. We render the lines ahead of time and play the audio files, so there's nothing to go wrong while filming.

### 4. The phone call (a human makes the decision)

If the intruder doesn't leave, the on-call property manager's actual phone rings. A real call, placed through **Phonely**, another sponsor. The AI describes the scene out loud and offers three choices:

> "Press one to send a guard. Press two to notify police. Press three to dismiss."

The manager picks one. That choice instantly appears on the dashboard.

This is the part that makes Warden a *service* and not software. We don't send an alert and hope someone reads it. We call a human and get a decision.

If the manager picks "send a guard," we check with **VOYGR** (a third sponsor) that the security company we're about to call is a real, currently-operating business — because dispatching to a vendor that closed last month is a real failure mode.

### 5. The paperwork (this is the actual moat)

One click produces a printable report. It looks nothing like the dashboard — black text on white paper, serif font, deliberately boring, like something filed with a court.

It contains:

- Which property, which unit, who owns it, who manages it
- **The lease status**, prominently: "vacant, turnover day 12 of 21" — this is the line that matters in an eviction case
- The photos, each stamped with the exact time *and timezone*, and a fingerprint (hash) of the image file proving it wasn't edited
- Exactly what the AI said, in its own raw words, not our summary
- Every action taken and who took it — the system, or the named manager who was logged in
- How it ended, and a signed statement that the record was created automatically at the time of the event

The one line we say about it: *"This document is how a squatter gets evicted and how an insurance claim gets paid."*

Everyone else sells you cameras. We hand you the evidence.

## Why this wins as a business (the 30-second version)

Every incident ends with a label: false alarm, left after the voice warning, manager dismissed it, guard sent, police called, eviction filed, claim paid.

Nobody else has those labels. Camera companies see pixels and never find out what happened next. We see the ending, because we're the ones handling it.

Those labels are worth three things: they teach the system when to bother a human (which is literally our profit margin), they're impossible for a competitor to copy, and eventually they're the dataset that lets insurers price this risk.

> "Competitors see pixels. Because we deliver the service, we see outcomes — and outcomes are what train the model, set the margin, and price the insurance."

## What the video looks like

Target: **about 2 minutes.** Shot in pieces and edited together — not one continuous take.

**Shot 1 — cold open, presenter to camera (10 sec)**
> "A 24/7 security guard post costs about two hundred thousand dollars a year. Here's what replaces it."

**Shot 2 — the dashboard, screen recording (10 sec)**
Dark ops screen. Six properties down the left. One live camera in the middle.
> "Right now we're watching forty thousand doors. This one — Maple Grove, Unit 4B — has been empty for twelve days."

**Shot 3 — the intruder (10 sec)**
One of us walks up to the camera and tries the door handle. **We film this ourselves.** No footage from the internet — it's someone else's copyright, someone else's real crime, and a judge who recognizes the clip stops believing everything else in the video.

**Shot 4 — the detection, screen recording (15 sec)**
A red sweep crosses the camera feed. A card slides in: **THREAT 4 — "one adult, attempting door, no uniform."** Then say nothing for one full second and let it land.
> "That's not a scripted alert. That's Claude looking at the actual frames and describing what it sees."

**Shot 5 — the voice (10 sec)**
The speaker line plays out loud. Our "intruder" freezes and backs away. Rehearse this beat — it's the most satisfying moment in the video.

**Shot 6 — the phone call (25 sec)**
The presenter's phone rings for real. They answer on camera, we hear the AI describe the scene, and they press 3. The dashboard flips to **DISMISSED BY MANAGER** while they're still holding the phone.
> "We don't hand you an alert. We make the decision."

**Shot 7 — the report (20 sec)**
Click Incident Report. The document appears in the print preview. Scroll slowly through the photos, the timestamps, the raw AI output, the signature block.
> "Timestamped frames. Every action logged. This document is how a squatter gets evicted and how an insurance claim gets paid."

**Shot 8 — close, presenter to camera (20 sec)**
The moat line, then:
> "That was a two-hundred-thousand-dollar-a-year guard post, replaced. Now imagine forty thousand doors."

## What's real and what's fake (we say this out loud)

Being straight about this is not a weakness. A judge who catches an unlabeled fake stops trusting the whole demo. A judge who watches us label it ourselves reads it as confidence.

**Real:**
- The camera loop and the AI classification. Genuinely live, running on our own footage.
- The phone call. An actual call to an actual phone.
- The vendor check through VOYGR.
- The login and operator identity through Hexclave.
- The voice, generated by Miso One.
- The report, generated from the real incident data.

**Simulated, and labeled that way on screen:**
- The property management system feed. The logic that turns lease status into alert sensitivity is real code; the connection to Yardi is not.
- Actually dispatching a guard. We validate the vendor is real, then stop there.

**Not claimed at all:**
- We have not trained a model on outcome labels. We built the system that *captures* them. If asked: "Tonight we built the thing that produces the training data. The training set is what month one of the service produces."

Any market statistic marked `[verify]` in `prd.md` does not get spoken in the video.

- **Person B — the screen.** The dashboard, the six properties, the threat card, the login, and the VOYGR vendor check.

Detailed file-by-file assignments are in the plan file, so nobody edits the same file as anybody else.

## How the video changes things (all in our favor)

1. **We can record each piece the moment it works.** No need for everything to succeed at once in one nerve-wracking take.
2. **We get as many takes as we want** of the flaky parts — the intruder walking in, the phone ringing.
3. **Start a screen recorder early and leave it running.** We'll accidentally capture our best take of the pipeline working while testing it.
4. **Record the narration separately** in a quiet spot and lay it over the footage. Much cleaner than talking over a noisy room.
5. **The one thing we can't fake is the AI output.** Real JSON arriving from Claude on screen is more convincing than any mockup — and it's not extra work, it's just recording what we're already testing.

## Before we film

- The camera loop produces real threat cards from our own staged footage
- The phone call connects and the answer reaches the dashboard
- The report renders from a real incident, with **no "localhost" printed in the page margins**
- Audio is audible in the recording
- Every timestamp on screen shows its timezone — it's an evidence product, the pedantry is the point
- No emoji anywhere in the interface
